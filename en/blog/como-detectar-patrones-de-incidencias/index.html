How to detect incident patterns

By Claudia · October 21, 2025 · 5 min read

The ability to identify incident patterns in production is one of the major differentiators between a plant that reacts late to problems and one that anticipates them. 

Simply recording incidents in isolation is not enough: what is truly valuable is analyzing them to uncover trends, recurring critical points and root causes that make it possible to prevent future problems. When incidents become structured data, companies can turn that information into strategic decisions that optimize production, reduce costs and improve quality.

In this article we will explore what incident patterns in production are, what data is needed to detect them, what analysis tools should be applied, how to put the results into practice and how platforms like Solved make this task simple and efficient.

What incident patterns in production are

The incident patterns in production are trends or repetitions that emerge when analyzing multiple incidents recorded at a plant. Detecting them means looking beyond each isolated case and finding connections between variables such as machines, shifts, production lines, owners or defect types.

Some common examples include:

  • A machine that shows similar failures recurring every week.
  • A shift where more incidents are concentrated than in the rest.
  • A type of quality defect that appears repeatedly across different batches.

Recognizing these patterns is essential for moving from reactive management (fixing what happens) to proactive management (preventing what could happen).

Data needed to detect patterns

To identify incident patterns in production, simply recording on the surface is not enough. Complete, consistent and well-structured data is required. The main ones are:

  • Dates and times for detection: they help identify shifts, cycles and critical moments.
  • Incident categories: quality, maintenance, safety, logistics, etc.
  • Assigned owners: makes it easier to see who is involved most often and how they respond.
  • Resolution times: show where delays are generated.
  • Frequency: how many times the same type of incident occurs.
  • Associated costs: production losses, rework or returns.

The quality and consistency of the data is key. If each operator records things differently, or if there are duplications or missing details, the patterns will not be reliable and the analysis will lose value.

Analysis tools

Detecting incident production patterns requires turning data into visual, practical information. The most useful tools are:

  • Charts and dashboards: they show trends over time, comparisons between lines or the evolution of defects.
  • Filters by variables: analyze incidents by shift, machine, batch, supplier or owner.
  • Cross comparisons: identify whether certain failures coincide with specific equipment or shifts.
  • Automatic alerts: set up notifications when a type of incident repeats more than expected.

This type of analysis turns records into actionable information for supervisors, quality managers and management.

How to apply pattern analysis to production

Detecting incident patterns in production is only the first step. What matters is turning the conclusions into concrete actions within the plant:

  • Maintenance planning: if a machine shows recurring failures, preventive maintenance can be scheduled before the next stoppage occurs.
  • Process review: a frequent defect may indicate that the production process needs adjustments to parameters or checks.
  • Targeted operator training: if patterns show errors concentrated in one shift, it may be necessary to train staff in critical procedures.
  • Prevention of non-conformities: by identifying incidents before they escalate, rejections in audits and certifications are reduced.

In this sense, patterns become a strategic tool for continuous improvement.

Benefits of pattern analysis

Implementing a system to analyze incident patterns in production offers clear benefits on several levels:

  • Reduction of repeated failures: by identifying root causes, incidents that used to repeat without a definitive solution are avoided.
  • Continuous improvement and organizational learning: teams learn from the data and refine their processes.
  • Cost and time savings: fewer incidents means fewer production stoppages, less rework and less material waste.
  • Audit readiness: the patterns detected make it possible to show auditors that the plant doesn't just react, it prevents problems.

Proactivity translates into efficiency, quality and competitiveness in the market.

Common challenges

Although the analysis of incident patterns in production is essential, it is not without difficulties. Among the most common challenges are:

  • Incomplete or duplicate data: if incident information is not properly recorded, the analysis loses value.
  • Misreading trends: confusing correlation with causation can lead to wrong decisions.
  • Lack of an analysis culture: some plants still don't see data analysis as part of continuous improvement, and limit themselves to “firefighting”.

Overcoming these challenges calls for a cultural change and the support of technological tools that simplify the task.
