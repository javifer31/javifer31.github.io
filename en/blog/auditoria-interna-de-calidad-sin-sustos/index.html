Internal quality audit: guide and checklist

By Claudia · July 29, 2026 · 6 min read

Internal quality audit without surprises

The internal quality audit is one of the most powerful tools a quality manager in the food industry has at their disposal. And yet, it still generates some tension in teams whenever the date approaches. The good news is that, with solid planning and the support of the right technology, this process can become a comfortable, useful, and even revealing routine. In this article we explain how to do it step by step, without last-minute rushes and without unpleasant surprises.

What an internal quality audit is for

Before discussing how to carry out an internal audit, it's worth being clear on what it's for. An internal audit is not a punitive inspection or a bureaucratic formality to get through. It is, in essence, a self-assessment exercise: an opportunity for the organization itself to check whether its processes meet the established requirements, whether procedures are correctly applied, and whether the quality management system is working as it should.

In the food industry, this practice is especially relevant. Companies must ensure compliance not only with their own internal standards, but also with current legislation and the requirements of standards such as ISO 9001 and ISO 22000 (ISO, n.d.), BRCGS (BRCGS, n.d.) or IFS, among others. Internal audits are precisely the mechanism that makes it possible to detect deviations before they become serious non-conformities or, worse still, issues that affect food safety.

In addition, a well-executed internal audit provides valuable input for continuous improvement: it identifies inefficiencies, detects latent risks, and generates objective evidence that strengthens the quality culture across the organization.

How to plan it and prepare the checklist

The success of an internal audit begins long before the day it takes place. Planning is key. The first step is to establish an internal audit program annual one, defining the processes or areas to be audited, the frequency, the responsible auditors, and the schedule. This program must take into account the criticality of each process, the results of previous audits, and any significant changes in the organization.

If your company works under the ISO 9001, this program is an explicit requirement. The standard requires internal audits to be planned, carried out, and documented systematically, covering all the processes of the management system over time. You can find more information on the specific requirements in our article on ISO 9001 internal audit.

Once the program is defined, it's time to prepare the checklist or checklist. This document is the auditor's compass during the visit: it gathers all the questions, evidence to review, and evaluation criteria. A good checklist should:

  • Be based on the applicable requirements (standard, legislation, internal procedures).
  • Cover both the documentation and actual practice in the plant.
  • Be detailed enough to leave no blind spots, without turning into an endless questionnaire.
  • Be tailored to the specific process or area being audited.

Preparing the checklist in advance also lets the auditee know what will be reviewed and have the necessary documentation ready, which reduces stress and greatly speeds up the day.

Execution, findings, and report

On the day of the audit, the auditor's job is to gather objective evidence to assess whether the system works as intended. This involves reviewing records and documents, observing practices on the plant floor, interviewing the staff involved, and checking what is said against what is actually done.

During execution, it's essential to maintain a professional, constructive attitude. The goal is not to find someone to blame, but to identify opportunities for improvement. An effective internal auditor knows how to ask the right questions, listens actively, and records findings accurately and objectively.

Findings can be classified into several categories: conformities, non-conformities (major or minor), and observations or areas for improvement. Each finding must be backed by concrete evidence: a missing record, a procedure that isn't followed, a documented out-of-range temperature. Without evidence, there is no valid finding.

At the end of the audit, the auditor prepares the audit report, a document that covers the scope, methodology, findings identified, associated evidence, and overall conclusions. This report is the basis for the corrective actions that will follow from the process and is an essential record of the management system.

It's worth remembering that, as set out in the Regulation (EC) No 852/2004 on the hygiene of foodstuffs (Regulation [EC] 852/2004, 2004), food business operators must implement and maintain procedures based on HACCP principles and demonstrate their effectiveness with documentation. Internal audits are one of the mechanisms that allow this compliance to be evidenced systematically.

An internal quality audit doesn't have to be a stressful moment. With the right planning, the right checklists, a well-trained team, and a tool like Solved that centralizes the entire process, it can become one of the most valuable assets in your quality system. The stress disappears when the process is under control.
