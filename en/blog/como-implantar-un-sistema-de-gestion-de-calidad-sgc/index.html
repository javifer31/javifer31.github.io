How to roll out a quality management system (QMS)

If you work in the food industry, you know that regulatory requirements, pressure from major retailers and consumer expectations leave no room for improvisation. Having a food quality management system solid one is, nowadays, an essential condition for operating with guarantees, earning your customers' trust and standing out in an increasingly competitive market. In this article we explain exactly what a QMS is, how to roll it out step by step and how a solution like Solved can make that path much simpler.

What a quality management system (QMS) is and what it brings to a food company

A quality management system (QMS) is the set of policies, processes, procedures and records that an organization establishes to ensure its products and services systematically meet legal, regulatory and customer requirements. In the food industry, the QMS goes beyond mere certification: it is the backbone that supports the safety, legality and continuous improvement of the entire operation.

A properly rolled-out QMS delivers concrete, measurable benefits. First, it drastically reduces the risk of food safety incidents, since checks are defined, assigned and verified. Second, it facilitates access to major distribution chains and industrial customers that require certifications such as the main quality standards and regulations in the sector. Third, it turns quality into a real competitive advantage, by objectively and verifiably demonstrating that your company produces with rigor. Finally, it builds an internal culture geared toward prevention and continuous improvement, which translates into fewer reworks, fewer returns and greater operational efficiency.

The legal basis for any QMS in the food sector starts within the European framework. As established by the Regulation (EC) No 178/2002 laying down the general principles and requirements of food law (Regulation [EC] 178/2002, 2002), food business operators are primarily responsible for ensuring that the food they market meets safety requirements. A structured QMS is precisely the tool that makes this demonstrable.

Steps to roll out a quality management system in the food industry

Rolling out a QMS can seem like a monumental task, but if approached in an orderly way and with the right support, the process is perfectly manageable. These are the fundamental steps:

1. Initial diagnosis and gap analysis. Before designing anything, it is essential to know the starting point. A gap analysis compares the company's current situation with the requirements of the chosen standard or reference, whether that's ISO 22000 or ISO 9001 standard (ISO, n.d.), BRC, IFS or FSSC 22000. This diagnosis makes it possible to prioritize actions and calculate the real effort required.

2. Defining the scope and the quality policy. The scope defines which products, processes and facilities are covered by the QMS. The quality policy sets out management's commitment and strategic objectives. Without the explicit backing of senior management, no system survives in the long run.

3. Hazard identification and analysis (HACCP). In the food sector, the QMS necessarily incorporates the Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points (HACCP) system. This analysis identifies the biological, chemical and physical risks of each process and defines the checks needed to eliminate them or reduce them to acceptable levels.

4. Designing and rolling out procedures and instructions. Each key process must be documented: how it's done, who does it, how often and how it's recorded. This is where many quality managers feel the heaviest bureaucratic burden, especially if they work with paper documents or scattered files.

5. Training and internal communication. The QMS only works if the people working on the plant floor know it and apply it. Ongoing training and clear communication of procedures are as important as the documents themselves.

6. Monitoring, measurement and internal audits. Once rolled out, the system needs to be verified periodically. Internal audits make it possible to detect deviations before the external auditor arrives and trigger the continuous improvement cycle.

7. Management review and continuous improvement. Management must periodically review the QMS results — incidents, non-conformities, objectives — to make strategic decisions that keep the system alive and effective.

The documentation, records and audits you need

Documentation is the visible skeleton of any quality management system in the food industry. Without it, there is no traceability, no possible audit and no certification. But managing it efficiently is one of the biggest day-to-day challenges.

A complete QMS requires, at a minimum, the following documented elements: the quality policy and objectives, the system manual (where required by the chosen reference), standard operating procedures (SOPs), work instructions, HACCP plans and hygiene control plans, and the records that provide evidence of every check carried out.

As for records, their value is twofold: on the one hand, they demonstrate to an auditor that checks are actually carried out; on the other, they are a data source for detecting trends, anomalies and opportunities for improvement. The most common records include temperature checks, microbiological and physicochemical analyses, cleaning and disinfection records, raw material receiving checks, and the results of internal and external audits.

As for audits, a mature QMS involves at least three types: internal audits (carried out by the quality team itself), second-party audits (carried out by customers) and third-party audits (carried out by accredited certification bodies). Preparing for and passing these processes competently requires keeping documentation accessible, up to date and organized at all times.
