7 quality control KPIs that actually matter

In the food industry, managing quality without concrete data is like driving with your eyes closed. The quality control KPIs are the compass that lets quality managers make informed decisions, detect deviations before they become bigger problems, and demonstrate the department's real value to management. However, not all indicators provide the same value: choosing the right ones is what separates a reactive management system from a truly preventive one.

What a quality KPI is and why you should measure it

A quality KPI (Key Performance Indicator) is a quantifiable metric that reflects the performance of processes related to food safety, compliance, and continuous improvement in a food company. It isn't about accumulating data for its own sake, but about selecting the quality indicators that actually guide decision-making.

Measuring is the foundation of any solid management system. As established by the Regulation (EC) No 178/2002 laying down the general principles and requirements of food law (Regulation [EC] 178/2002, 2002), food business operators must ensure traceability and checks at every stage of production, processing, and distribution. Without well-defined indicators, meeting this requirement becomes an almost impossible task.

Food quality KPIs also make it easier to communicate with other departments and senior management, since they translate the technical complexity of quality work into figures that are easy to understand and compare. They are also essential during internal and external audits, where numerical evidence is indispensable.

The 7 essential quality control KPIs

Below are the seven indicators every quality manager in the food industry should track systematically.

1. Number of non-conformities recorded
This indicator measures how many deviations from defined standards occur in a given period. It can be broken down by area, process, or incident type. A sustained increase in non-conformities signals that something isn't working properly in the system; a progressive reduction indicates that corrective measures are having an effect.

2. Average non-conformity resolution time
Detecting problems isn't enough: they need to be resolved quickly. This KPI measures the time elapsed between opening a non-conformity and its effective closure. Long resolution times can point to bottlenecks in assigning responsibilities or implementing corrective actions. Reducing this indicator is key to minimizing the impact of deviations on production and product safety.

3. Incidents per batch or reference
This quality indicator links the number of incidents detected to each batch or product reference. It helps identify whether problems are concentrated in specific raw materials, particular production lines, or certain suppliers. It's especially valuable for directing improvement efforts toward the highest-risk points.

4. Audit compliance percentage
Measures the extent to which audited processes and procedures meet established requirements, whether internal or derived from standards such as ISO 22000 and ISO 9001 (ISO, n.d.). A high compliance percentage reflects the maturity of the management system; a low one points to priority areas for improvement. This KPI also allows you to compare progress across audit cycles and justify investment in training or resources.

5. Customer complaint rate
Complaints are a valuable source of external information. This KPI measures the number of complaints received relative to the volume of product delivered or sold. A high rate can point to failures in final quality control, labeling, the cold chain, or allergen management, among other factors. Reducing it directly impacts customer satisfaction and the company's reputation.

6. Effectiveness of corrective actions
Not every corrective action permanently eliminates the problem. This indicator measures what percentage of implemented actions has effectively resolved the non-conformity, with no recurrence within a defined period. Low effectiveness suggests that the root cause analysis isn't thorough enough or that the solutions adopted don't address the underlying problem.

7. Percentage of records completed on time
Control records —temperatures, cleaning, CCP checks, analytical tests— are the backbone of quality documentation. Measuring what proportion of these records is completed within the set deadline helps detect gaps in operational discipline and act before an audit reveals documentation gaps.

How to set realistic targets for your quality metrics

Setting targets is as important as choosing the indicators. A poorly calibrated target can frustrate the team or, at the other extreme, create a false sense of control. To set realistic targets, it's advisable to follow these criteria:

Start from the current baseline. Before setting a target, analyze the history of the last six to twelve months. Without reference data, any target is arbitrary.

Apply the SMART methodology. Targets must be specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound. For example: "Reduce the average non-conformity resolution time from 12 to 8 days next quarter" is a SMART target; "improve quality" is not.

Involve the operational teams. Production, logistics, and maintenance managers are key players in meeting food quality KPIs. Setting targets together builds commitment and makes it easier to identify real barriers.

Review and adjust periodically. A target that seemed ambitious six months ago may now be outdated after rolling out a new production line or changing supplier. Quarterly or half-yearly reviews keep KPIs aligned with operational reality.
