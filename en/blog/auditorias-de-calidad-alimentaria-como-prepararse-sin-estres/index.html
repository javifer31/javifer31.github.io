Food quality audits: how to prepare without stress

For any quality manager in the food industry, the food quality audits represent one of the most stressful moments of the year. Weeks of document review, record review, evidence gathering… and the constant feeling that something might slip through. However, with the right strategy and the right tools, it's perfectly possible to go into an external audit with confidence and no surprises. In this article we'll tell you how to achieve that.

What auditors look for in a food company

Before preparing for any audit, it's essential to understand what an external auditor actually assesses when they walk into your plant. It's not just about checking that your paperwork is in order: auditors want to see that your quality management system is real, consistent and alive in the organization's day-to-day.

In general terms, any quality audit food audit —whether IFS Food, BRC/BRCGS o FSSC 22000— assesses the same major pillars:

  • Document management: up-to-date procedures, complete and traceable records, current versions of key documents.
  • HACCP system: updated hazard analysis, defined and monitored critical control points, documented corrective actions.
  • Prerequisites: pest control, cleaning and disinfection, facility maintenance, water control, personnel hygiene.
  • Traceability: the ability to trace a batch from raw material to finished product and back again.
  • Non-conformity and corrective action management: evidence that problems are detected, analyzed and resolved systematically.
  • Management involvement: auditors verify that the quality culture runs through the whole company, not just the quality department.

The Regulation (EC) No 852/2004 on the hygiene of foodstuffs (Regulation [EC] 852/2004, 2004) establishes the obligation to implement and maintain procedures based on HACCP principles, which forms the regulatory foundation on which all private certification standards are built.

Knowing these pillars lets you approach preparation strategically, prioritizing the areas that carry the most weight in the final score and those where deviations have historically appeared.

The most common mistakes in IFS and BRC food audits

Preparing for an IFS audit or a BRC food audit requires knowing the most common failures. Spotting them in advance lets you act before the auditor finds them. These are the most frequent:

1. Outdated or inconsistent documentation

One of the most repeated findings in any food quality audit is procedures with a version earlier than the one being applied on the plant floor, or records that don't match the documented instructions. For an auditor, a mismatch between what the paper says and what actually happens is a sign that the system isn't under control.

2. Incomplete records or incorrect data

Unsigned temperatures, CCP checks with blank boxes, cleaning records with impossible dates… Records are the main source of evidence during an audit, and any gap can turn into a major non-conformity. A well-designed checklist quality audit reviewed regularly, is one of the best defenses against this type of mistake.

3. Corrective actions without closure or verified effectiveness

Opening a non-conformity is only the first step. IFS and BRC auditors require proof that corrective actions have been implemented, that their effectiveness has been verified, and that there's documented evidence of the whole process. Non-conformities that are opened and never closed are one of the most common causes of penalties.

4. HACCP not reviewed for too long

Hazard analysis has to stay alive and be updated whenever the production process, legislation or ingredients change. Finding a HACCP plan that hasn't been touched in years is an immediate red flag. According to the BRCGS Global Standards (BRCGS, n.d.), periodic review of the HACCP system is a fundamental requirement for keeping certification.

5. Lack of documented staff training

It's not enough for operators to know the procedures: it has to be demonstrated. Training records, competency assessments and evidence of internal communication are documents the auditor will almost certainly request.

6. Poor traceability

A traceability exercise is common in certification audits. If the quality manager takes more than four hours to reconstruct the full path of a batch —forward and backward— the company runs the risk of a critical non-conformity.
