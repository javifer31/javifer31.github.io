Quality audit without errors or surprises

If you work in the food industry, the word quality audit probably triggers a mix of respect and vertigo. However, far from being a dreaded formality, a well-prepared audit is one of the most valuable tools for ensuring food safety, improving internal processes and showing customers and regulatory bodies that your company works with rigor. In this article we explain what it is, what it's for and how to approach it without errors or surprises.

What a quality audit is and what it's for

A quality audit is a systematic, documented and independent process that assesses to what extent an organization meets the requirements set out in its quality management system, whether regulatory, legal or internal. It is not a one-off inspection, but a structured analysis that examines procedures, records, facilities and actual practices.

What is a quality audit for? Its main objectives are several: detecting deviations before they become serious problems, identifying opportunities for continuous improvement, verifying compliance with current legislation and reinforcing the trust of customers and business partners. Ultimately, a quality management audit acts as a mirror that objectively shows the real state of the system.

In the food industry, this process takes on special significance because it is directly linked to consumer safety. As established by the Regulation (EC) No 178/2002 laying down the general principles and requirements of food law (Regulation [EC] 178/2002, 2002), food business operators are primarily responsible for ensuring that the products they market meet safety requirements. Audits are precisely the mechanism that makes it possible to verify that this responsibility is being exercised effectively.

Types of quality audits: internal, external and supplier

Not all quality audits are the same. Depending on who carries them out and what their scope is, we can distinguish three main types. If you want to go deeper into their differences, we recommend reading our article on types of quality audit.

Internal audit: It is carried out by staff from the organization itself or auditors hired for that purpose, who act independently of the area being audited. Its goal is to assess how the management system works before a third party does. It is the perfect tool for continuous improvement and for preparing for external certifications. Standards such as ISO 22000 and ISO 9001 (ISO, n.d.) require organizations to carry out internal audits at planned intervals to ensure that the system conforms and is effective.

External or second-/third-party audit: It is carried out by customers (second party) or accredited certification bodies (third party). This type of audit is what leads to internationally recognized certifications such as BRCGS, IFS or FSSC 22000, which open doors to large distribution chains and international markets.

Supplier audit: Food companies depend on an extensive supply chain. Auditing critical suppliers makes it possible to verify that the raw materials or services received meet the required standards, reducing the risk of non-conformities entering the production process.

Phases of the audit process: from plan to report

A well-executed quality audit follows a clear path. Knowing its phases makes it possible to plan ahead, organize documentation in good time and avoid last-minute rushing.

1. Planning: It all starts with drawing up the audit plan. At this stage the scope, objectives, criteria to be assessed, schedule and audit team are defined. A detailed plan reduces improvisation and ensures no area is left unreviewed.

2. Preparation and document review: Before the on-site visit, the audit team reviews existing documentation: procedures, records, results of previous audits and corrective actions. This prior phase makes it possible to detect inconsistencies on paper and focus the audit's time on critical points.

3. Execution: This is the field work phase. Auditors directly observe facilities and processes, interview staff and verify that actual practice matches what is documented. This is where findings are identified: conformities, non-conformities and opportunities for improvement.

4. Communication of findings: At the end of the audit a closing meeting is held where the findings are presented to the company's team. Transparency at this stage is essential so that those responsible understand the problems detected and can act quickly.

5. Audit report: The auditor draws up a formal report with all the classified findings, the evidence backing them and the corresponding recommendations. This document is the basis for the corrective action plan.

6. Follow-up and closure: An audit doesn't end with the report. The quality manager must manage the non-conformities detected, implement corrective actions and verify their effectiveness within the agreed due dates. Without this phase, the audit loses much of its value.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them by digitizing checklists and findings

Even the most experienced quality teams can fall into certain mistakes that compromise the value of quality audits. Identifying them is the first step to overcoming them.

Insufficient preparation: One of the most common failures is not spending the necessary time reviewing documentation before the audit. Working with outdated records or procedures that don't reflect the reality of the process generates avoidable findings. The solution is to keep the management system alive all year round, not only when the audit date is approaching.

Paper checklists that are hard to manage: Paper checklists get lost, deteriorate and are hard to analyze in aggregate. When the time comes to demonstrate compliance to an external auditor, finding the right evidence among folders and physical files can become a nightmare. Digitizing checklists makes it possible to access information instantly, filter by area or process and show the compliance history in a couple of clicks.

Reactive management of findings: Noting down a non-conformity and forgetting about it until the next audit is a mistake that's repeated too often. A digital findings management system assigns owners, sets due dates and sends automatic reminders, ensuring corrective actions are closed on time and as required.

Lack of traceability in evidence: During an external audit, the auditor may request evidence from any time of year. Without a centralized tool, retrieving that specific record can take hours. With a digital platform like Solved, all documentation stays linked to the corresponding process and can be retrieved in seconds.

Poor communication between departments: Quality audits aren't just a matter for the quality department. Production, logistics, purchasing and maintenance are also assessed. When communication between areas fails, inconsistencies appear between what an owner says and what the documentation shows. Involving every department in the everyday quality culture is key to avoiding these situations.

In short, a quality audit without errors or surprises isn't a matter of luck: it's the result of ongoing preparation, organized document management and the support of digital tools that make the quality manager's daily work easier. Technology doesn't replace professional judgment, but it does remove unnecessary bureaucracy and free up time for what really matters: improving the system and ensuring food safety.
